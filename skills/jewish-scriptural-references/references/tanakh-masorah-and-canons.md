# Tanakh, Masorah, and Biblical Canons

A rigorous reference guide to the structure, manuscript traditions, Masoretic notes, and versification of the Hebrew Bible (*Tanakh*).

---

## 1. The Tripartite Canon: The 24 Books of Tanakh

In Jewish tradition, the Hebrew Bible is called **Tanakh** (תנ״ך), an acronym formed from its three primary divisions:
1. **Torah (תּוֹרָה)** — Teaching / Law / Instruction (5 books)
2. **Nevi'im (נְבִיאִים)** — Prophets (8 books)
3. **Ketuvim (כְּתוּבִים)** — Writings (11 books)

Total: **24 Books** (in contrast to the 39 books of the Protestant Old Testament, which divides Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra-Nehemiah, and the Twelve into separate books).

## 2. The Masoretic Text (MT) Tradition

The authoritative Hebrew text of the Tanakh preserved by Jewish scribes (*Soferim*) and scholars (*Masoretes*) between the 6th and 10th centuries CE:

### Key Manuscripts
1. **The Aleppo Codex (*Keter Aram Tzova*)** (c. 920 CE): The pinnacle of Tiberian Masoretic scholarship, vocalized and annotated by Aaron ben Moses ben Asher. Praised by Maimonides for its exactitude. Today approximately one-third of the text (including most of the Torah) is missing due to anti-Jewish riots in Aleppo in 1947.
2. **The Leningrad Codex (B19a)** (1008 CE): The oldest complete surviving manuscript of the entire Hebrew Bible based on the Ben Asher tradition. It serves as the primary text for modern critical editions (BHS, BHQ) and the digital Sefaria Miqra.

### Scribing Components
- **Consonantal Text (*K'tiv*)**: The raw unvocalized letters written on Torah scrolls (*Sefer Torah*).
- **Vocalization (*Nikkud*)**: Vowel points developed by the Tiberian Masoretes.
- **Cantillation (*Te'amim* / *Trop*)**: Musical notation that simultaneously governs phrasing, syntax, and punctuation.
- **Masorah Parva & Masorah Magna**: Marginal notes recording word frequencies, hapax legomena (*leit*), and differences between what is written (*K'tiv*) and what is read (*Q're*).

## 3. Versification Discrepancies: Tanakh (MT) vs Christian Bibles

Chapter divisions were introduced into Latin Bibles by Stephen Langton in the 13th century and adopted into Hebrew printed editions by Daniel Bomberg in Venice (1517–1525). However, significant discrepancies exist between Hebrew verse divisions and English Christian translations:

### 1. Psalms Superscriptions
In the Hebrew Masoretic Text, the title or dedication of a psalm (e.g., *Mizmor l'David*) is counted as **verse 1**. In many Christian English translations (KJV, ESV, NIV), the superscription is left unnumbered, causing Hebrew verse numbers to be **one verse ahead** of English Bibles:
- **Hebrew MT**: Psalm 23:1 is *Mizmor l'David: Adonai ro'i lo echsar...* (contains both superscription and opening declaration).
- **English Bibles**: Superscription unnumbered; Verse 1 begins *"The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."*
- **Example in Psalm 51**: MT has 21 verses (vv. 1–2 are superscription concerning Nathan the prophet); English Bibles have 19 verses (English 51:1 = MT 51:3).

### 2. Major Versification Shifts in the Prophets

| Book & Passage | Masoretic Text (MT) | Christian Editions (KJV, ESV) | Nature of Divergence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Malachi** | Malachi 3:1–24 | Malachi 3:1–18; **Malachi 4:1–6** | MT combines Christian ch. 4 as Malachi 3:19–24 |
| **Hosea** | Hosea 14:2–10 | **Hosea 14:1–9** | MT 14:1 is opening call; Christian shifted by 1 |
| **Joel** | Joel 3:1–5; 4:1–21 | **Joel 2:28–32; 3:1–21** | Joel 3 in MT is Christian 2:28–32 ("pour out my Spirit") |
| **Jonah** | Jonah 2:1–11 | **Jonah 1:17; 2:1–10** | Jonah 2:1 in MT is Christian 1:17 ("great fish swallowed Jonah") |
| **Exodus** | Exodus 20:1–14 | Exodus 20:1–17 | Decalogue (Ten Commandments) verse grouping differences |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> When quoting Hebrew texts alongside English translations, always identify whether the citation uses **Masoretic versification** or **Christian chapter:verse numbering**.

## 4. Weekly Torah Portions (*Parashiyot*)

The Torah is divided into **54 Parashiyot** (weekly portions read publicly on Shabbat):
- **Genesis (Bereshit)**: 12 Parashiyot (Bereshit, Noach, Lekh-Lekha, Vayera, Chayei Sarah, Toldot, Vayetzei, Vayishlach, Vayeshev, Miketz, Vayigash, Vayechi).
- **Exodus (Shemot)**: 11 Parashiyot (Shemot, Va'era, Bo, Beshalach, Yitro, Mishpatim, Terumah, Tetzaveh, Ki Tisa, Vayakhel, Pekudei).
- **Leviticus (Vayikra)**: 10 Parashiyot (Vayikra, Tzav, Shemini, Tazria, Metzora, Acharei Mot, Kedoshim, Emor, Behar, Bechukotai).
- **Numbers (Bamidbar)**: 10 Parashiyot (Bamidbar, Nasso, Beha'alotkha, Shelach, Korach, Chukat, Balak, Pinchas, Matot, Masei).
- **Deuteronomy (Devarim)**: 11 Parashiyot (Devarim, Va'etchanan, Eikev, Re'eh, Shoftim, Ki Teitzei, Ki Tavo, Nitzavim, Vayelekh, Ha'azinu, V'Zot HaBerakhah).

Torah scrolls also feature ancient structural divisions:
- **Petuchah (פ)**: Open paragraph break (starts on a new line).
- **Setumah (ס)**: Closed paragraph break (starts on the same line after an indentation/gap).

## 5. Ancient Aramaic Translations (*Targumim*)

Before modern vernacular translations, the Jewish community utilized Aramaic translations during public synagogue readings:
- **Targum Onkelos**: The authoritative, literal Aramaic translation of the Torah. Paraphrases anthropomorphisms to protect divine transcendence.
- **Targum Yonatan ben Uzziel**: Tannaitic Aramaic translation of the Prophets (Nevi'im), containing extensive aggadic additions.
- **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan & Fragmentary Targums**: Palestinian Targumim to the Pentateuch rich in folklore and midrashic embellishments.
