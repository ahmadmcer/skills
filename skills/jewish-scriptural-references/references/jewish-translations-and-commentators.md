# Jewish Translations, Classical Commentators, and Hermeneutics

An exhaustive reference to major Jewish English Bible translations, classical medieval and early modern commentators (_Perushim_ / _Rishonim_), and rabbinic hermeneutical systems.

---

## 1. Major Jewish English Translations

## 2. Classical Biblical Commentators (_Perushim_ / _Rishonim_)

Jewish biblical study is traditionally practiced with _Mikraot Gedolot_ ("Rabbinic Bibles"), where the biblical text is surrounded by classical commentaries:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                   THE ARCHITECTURE OF MIKRAOT GEDOLOT                  │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                           [ Masoretic Text ]                           │
│                      [ Vocalization & Te'amim ]                        │
│                           [ Targum Onkelos ]                           │
│ ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── │
│ [ Rashi ]           [ Rashbam ]         [ Ibn Ezra ]         [ Ramban ] │
│ Plain & Midrash     Strict Context      Grammar & Science    Philosophy │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Major Rishonim (11th–15th Centuries)

1. **Rashi (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, 1040–1105, Troyes, France)**:
   - The foundational commentator on both Tanakh and the Babylonian Talmud.
   - **Methodology**: Declared goal is to establish the plain sense (_peshuto shel mikra_), but achieves this by masterfully selecting Midrashic aggadot that resolve grammatical, stylistic, or textual anomalies.
   - **Signature phrase**: _"I have come only to explain the simple meaning of the verse..."_ (_Ein li ella peshuto shel mikra_).

2. **Rashbam (Rabbi Shmuel ben Meir, c. 1085–1158, France)**:
   - Rashi's grandson and leading Tosafist.
   - **Methodology**: Committed to radical, uncompromising _Peshat_ (literal, contextual reading), explicitly stating that the plain meaning of Scripture can and does differ from halakhic rabbinic derivations.

3. **Ibn Ezra (Rabbi Avraham ibn Ezra, 1089–1167, Toledo, Spain)**:
   - Polymath, grammarian, astronomer, and poet.
   - **Methodology**: Scientific, philological, and grammatical rigor. Rejects ungrounded midrashim and mystical allegories; pioneer of biblical Hebrew grammar and proto-historical textual criticism.

4. **Ramban (Nachmanides, Rabbi Moshe ben Nachman, 1194–1270, Girona / Acre)**:
   - Legal titan, mystic, and defender of Judaism at the 1263 Disputation of Barcelona.
   - **Methodology**: Deep contextual peshat interwoven with psychological insights, historical realism, philosophical critiques of Maimonides and Ibn Ezra, and hints of Kabbalistic mysteries (_al pi derekh ha-emet_ - "according to the way of truth").

5. **Radak (Rabbi David Kimchi, 1160–1235, Provence)**:
   - Renowned Hebrew grammarian (_Sefer Mikhlol_).
   - **Methodology**: Clear, lucid grammatical explanations, especially revered for his commentary on Nevi'im (Prophets) and Tehillim (Psalms).

6. **Sforno (Rabbi Ovadia ben Yaakov Sforno, 1475–1550, Italy)**:
   - Renaissance physician and philosopher.
   - **Methodology**: Focuses on theological coherence, human moral responsibility, and free will.

7. **Kli Yakar (Rabbi Shlomo Ephraim of Luntschitz, 1550–1619, Prague)**:
   - Ethical and homiletic commentary on the Torah, emphasizing humility and critique of wealth.

8. **Or HaChayim (Rabbi Chaim ibn Attar, 1696–1743, Morocco / Jerusalem)**:
   - Mystical, halakhic, and moral commentary deeply revered in Sephardic and Hasidic traditions.

9. **Malbim (Meir Leibush ben Yehiel Michel Weiser, 1809–1879, Eastern Europe)**:
   - 19th-century commentator who established the principle that biblical Hebrew contains **zero true synonyms or redundancies**; every subtle variation in vocabulary or syntax reveals a precise legal or theological distinction.

## 3. The PaRDeS Hermeneutical Framework

Originating in Rabbinic antiquity and codified in medieval Kabbalistic literature (_Zohar_), the **PaRDeS** (פַּרְדֵּס - meaning "orchard") acronym represents the four ascending levels of scriptural exegesis:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        THE 4 TIERS OF PaRDeS                           │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ P - PESHAT (פְּשָׁט)  → Contextual, grammatical, literal sense         │
│ R - REMEZ (רֶמֶז)    → Allegorical, hinted, allusive, philosophical    │
│ D - DERASH (דְּרַשׁ)  → Homiletical, midrashic, ethical application     │
│ S - SOD (סוֹד)       → Mystical, esoteric, Kabbalistic insight         │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Application Example: Genesis 1:1 (_Bereshit bara Elohim..._)

- **Peshat**: Grammatical analysis of the construct state (_b'reishit_ - "in the beginning of..."). As Rashi and Ibn Ezra note, it denotes the beginning of God's creating, rather than an absolute chronological timestamp.
- **Remez**: The letter _Bet_ (בּ) opening the Torah is numerically 2 and closed on three sides, hinting that humanity may only investigate what came after creation, not what preceded it or lies above/below.
- **Derash**: Midrash Rabbah notes _Bereshit_ means "for the sake of _Reshit_" (the First): for the sake of the Torah (called _reshit darko_ in Prov 8:22) and Israel (called _reshit tevu'atah_ in Jer 2:3).
- **Sod**: The Zohar's exposition: Creation through the Ten Sefirot, beginning from the Primordial Point (_Keter/Chokhmah_) expanding into cosmic manifestation.

## 4. The 13 Hermeneutical Principles of Rabbi Yishmael

Found in the introduction to the _Sifra_ and recited daily in traditional morning prayers (_Shacharit_), these 13 logical deduction rules (_Middot_) govern how the Oral Law derives halakhah from the Written Torah:

1. **Kal va-chomer (קַל וָחֹמֶר)**: _A fortiori_ argument (minor to major premise).
2. **Gezerah shavah (גְּזֵרָה שָׁוָה)**: Equivalence of expressions (identical words in different passages linking laws, valid only via oral tradition).
3. **Binyan av (בִּנְיַן אָב)**: General principle constructed from one or two biblical verses.
4. **Kelal u-ferat (כְּלָל וּפְרָט)**: General statement followed by a specific enumeration (rule applies only to the specific).
5. **Perat u-khelal (פְּרָט וּכְלָל)**: Specific enumeration followed by a general term (rule applies to all similar cases).
6. **Kelal u-ferat u-khelal (כְּלָל וּפְרָט וּכְלָל)**: General, specific, general (governed by the nature of the specific).
7. General requiring a specific, or specific requiring a general.
8. Anything included in a general rule that is singled out to teach something new.
9. Anything included in a general rule singled out for a different treatment.
10. Anything included in a general rule singled out for a fresh provision.
11. Anything included in a general rule singled out and then restored.
12. A matter elucidated by its context (_davar ha-lamed me-inyano_) or subsequent statement (_mi-sofo_).
13. Two biblical passages contradicting one another until a third passage reconciles them.
