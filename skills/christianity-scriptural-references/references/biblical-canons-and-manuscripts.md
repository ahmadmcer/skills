# Biblical Canons & Manuscript Traditions

Read this guide when navigating biblical canons across Christian traditions, manuscript witnesses,
or the primary textual foundations of the Old and New Testaments.

---

## 1. The Three Major Biblical Canons

While all mainstream Christian traditions recognize the identical 27 books of the New Testament,
the Old Testament canon differs between Protestant, Catholic, and Eastern Orthodox traditions:

| Tradition            | Old Testament Count | New Testament Count | Total Books | Distinctive Books Included                                                                                                                                              |
| :------------------- | :-----------------: | :-----------------: | :---------: | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Protestant**       |         39          |         27          |   **66**    | Corresponds strictly to the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) canon codified in the Masoretic tradition.                                                                            |
| **Catholic**         |         46          |         27          |   **73**    | Includes the 7 Deuterocanonical books: Tobit, Judith, 1 & 2 Maccabees, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Baruch, plus additions to Daniel and Esther (Council of Trent, 1546). |
| **Eastern Orthodox** |        49–51        |         27          |  **76–78**  | Includes Catholic deuterocanon plus 3 Maccabees, 1 Esdras, Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151 (and 4 Maccabees in an appendix).                                              |

### The Terminology: Protocanonical vs. Deuterocanonical

- **Protocanonical**: Books whose authority was undisputed from the earliest centuries.
- **Deuterocanonical ("Second Canon")**: Books present in the ancient Greek Septuagint (LXX) that were affirmed in the Catholic/Orthodox traditions, but termed "Apocrypha" in Protestant traditions (considered edifying for history and morality, but not for establishing doctrine).

## 2. Primary Manuscript Witnesses for the Old Testament

1. **The Masoretic Text (MT)**:
   - The authoritative Hebrew and Aramaic text of the Tanakh, meticulously compiled and preserved by Jewish Masorete scholars (Ben Asher family) between the 7th and 10th centuries CE.
   - Primary codices: _Codex Leningradensis_ (1008 CE, oldest complete Hebrew manuscript; basis for _Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia / Quinta_) and the _Aleppo Codex_ (c. 920 CE).
2. **The Septuagint (LXX)**:
   - The ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures produced in Alexandria between the 3rd and 2nd centuries BCE.
   - The primary Scripture quoted by New Testament authors and the Early Church Fathers.
3. **The Dead Sea Scrolls (DSS)**:
   - Discovered at Qumran (1947), dating from 250 BCE to 68 CE.
   - Provided biblical Hebrew manuscripts a millennium older than the Leningrad Codex, confirming extraordinary fidelity in Masoretic transmission while illuminating ancient textual families.

## 3. Primary Manuscript Witnesses for the New Testament

The New Testament was written in **Koine Greek** and is witnessed by over 5,800 Greek manuscripts,
more than any other ancient literature.

### Major Codex Witnesses (Great Uncials)

- **Codex Sinaiticus ($\aleph$, 4th century)**: Contains the oldest complete New Testament, preserved in the British Library.
- **Codex Vaticanus (B, 4th century)**: Housed in the Vatican Library, one of the most reliable witnesses to the Alexandrian text-type.
- **Codex Alexandrinus (A, 5th century)**: Major uncial witness containing both Old and New Testaments.

### Text-Type Traditions

1. **Alexandrian Text-Type**: Characterized by brevity and austerity; represented by Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. Forms the foundation of modern Critical Texts.
2. **Byzantine (Majority) Text-Type**: Characterized by smooth readings and conflations; predominant in Greek manuscripts from the 9th to 16th centuries.
3. **Western Text-Type**: Characterized by paraphrastic expansion (e.g. Codex Bezae).

### Textus Receptus vs. Modern Critical Text

- **Textus Receptus ("Received Text")**: The Greek text edited by Desiderius Erasmus (1516), revised by Robert Stephanus and Theodore Beza. Served as the textual basis for the King James Version (KJV) and Reformation translations.
- **Critical Eclectic Text (Nestle-Aland NA28 / UBS 5th Edition)**: Rigorously reconstructs the earliest attainable text based on the oldest papyri (e.g. $\mathfrak{P}^{52}$, $\mathfrak{P}^{66}$, $\mathfrak{P}^{75}$) and early uncials. Serves as the basis for modern translations (ESV, NIV, NASB, NRSVue).
