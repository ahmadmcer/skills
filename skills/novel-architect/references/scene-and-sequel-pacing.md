# Scene and Sequel Micro-Pacing

A comprehensive technical guide to micro-narrative structure, cause-and-effect causality, and tension engineering based on **Dwight Swain** (_Techniques of the Selling Writer_) and **Jack Bickham** (_Scene & Structure_).

---

## 1. The Scene & Sequel Engine

Stories lose momentum and become episodic when writers link events with _"and then"_. To create an unbroken chain of narrative propulsion, every chapter is built from two alternating psychological units: **The Scene** (proactive, physical action) and **The Sequel** (reactive, emotional processing).

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        THE CAUSALITY ENGINE                            │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                        │
│   SCENE (Action):                                                      │
│     [ GOAL ] ───► [ CONFLICT ] ───► [ DISASTER / SETBACK ]             │
│                                              │                         │
│                                              ▼                         │
│   SEQUEL (Reaction):                                                   │
│     [ REACTION ] ───► [ DILEMMA ] ───► [ DECISION ]                    │
│                                              │                         │
│                                              ▼                         │
│                                   (Becomes Next Scene Goal)            │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

---

## 2. The Scene (The Proactive Unit)

A Scene is a dramatic unit in which the POV character pursues an immediate, tangible goal and encounters active resistance.

### 1. Goal (The Objective)

- Must be **immediate** (achievable within the scene itself).
- Must be **specific** and **observable** (e.g., _"Break into the vault"_, not _"Feel more confident"_).
- The stakes of failing to achieve the goal must be clear.

### 2. Conflict (The Obstacle)

- Opposition arising from another character, an environmental barrier, or systemic forces.
- The conflict must be dynamic: a series of tactical parries, escalations, or verbal thrusts.

### 3. Disaster (The Four Outcomes)

The climax of the scene must never end in an easy, flat victory. It must culminate in one of four distinct setbacks:

1. **"No"**: The character outright fails to achieve their goal.
2. **"No, and furthermore..."**: The character fails, and their attempt makes the situation drastically worse.
3. **"Yes, but..."**: The character achieves their goal, but the success introduces an unexpected, severe complication.
4. **"Yes, and therefore..."**: The character succeeds, but the action triggers immediate retaliation or new danger.

---

## 3. The Sequel (The Reactive Unit)

Following the Disaster, the character must process the emotional, physical, and tactical consequences before they can formulate a new plan.

### 1. Reaction (Emotional Aftermath)

- Visceral, physiological response: shock, fury, grief, exhaustion, panic.
- Internal monologue and sensory disorientation.

### 2. Dilemma (The Problem of No Good Choices)

- The character assesses their new, deteriorated circumstances.
- They realize there are no easy, painless solutions; all available paths involve sacrifice or danger.

### 3. Decision (The New Commitment)

- The character chooses the best of the flawed options.
- This active decision **crystallizes directly into the Goal of the next Scene**, creating an unbroken causal chain.

---

## 4. Pacing Control & Narrative Speed

Authors control the perceived speed and tension of their novel by modulating the ratio between Scenes and Sequels:

| Narrative Goal                                 | Strategy              | Implementation                                                                                                                                    |
| :--------------------------------------------- | :-------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **High Velocity (Climax / Thriller)**          | **Compress Sequels**  | Reduce the Sequel to a few sentences or heartbeats (instant Reaction &rarr; lightning Decision). Keep the story moving from Disaster to Disaster. |
| **Interiority / Resonance (Midpoint / Drama)** | **Expand Sequels**    | Dedicate full scenes to the Dilemma. Explore character backstory (_The Ghost_), thematic debate (_The Lie_), and relational intimacy.             |
| **Preventing Saggy Middle**                    | **Enforce Disasters** | Eliminate scenes where characters achieve goals without complications. Every scene must end with _"No, and furthermore"_ or _"Yes, but"_.         |

---

## 5. The Causality Rule: "Therefore / But"

Adopt the fundamental storytelling rule popularized by Trey Parker and Matt Stone:

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Inspect the connective tissue between your scenes:
>
> - If your scenes are connected by **"AND THEN"**, the story is episodic and drifting.
> - If your scenes are connected by **"THEREFORE"** (consequence) and **"BUT"** (complication), your narrative has momentum and tight causality.

## 6. The Standard Scene Card Schema

```markdown
### Scene [Number]: [Working Title]

- **POV Character**: [Name]
- **Unit Type**: Scene / Sequel / Hybrid
- **Target Word Count**: [e.g. 1,500 – 2,000 words]

#### For a Scene:

- **Scene Goal**: What tangible, immediate objective does the POV character want?
- **Active Conflict**: Who or what actively opposes them in this room/location?
- **Scene Disaster**: How does it fail? ("No" / "No, and furthermore" / "Yes, but")

#### For a Sequel:

- **Immediate Reaction**: What is the visceral, emotional shock?
- **Core Dilemma**: What are the limited, painful choices?
- **New Decision**: What new action do they commit to?
```
